# Cesena vs Emilia-Romagna Peers, 2008–2025: What the Data Show

_Living document, rewritten after the soundness review of 2026-10-30. Study overview and pitfalls: `studies/cesena/README.md`._

**How to read this document**
- **Everything is descriptive.** These are associations and orderings for one municipality, never causal claims. With 13 cities and one focal municipality, a rank is not statistical evidence.
- **Labels.** `robust` holds across the checks tried. `sensitive` changes with the period, sectors or pool, and all versions are shown. `context only` is background that cannot support a trend. `hypothesis` is an open idea, not a result. `retracted` was withdrawn after the review.
- **Conventions.** Euro figures are constant 2020 euro per resident unless marked nominal or total. Rank one is the highest of the group unless stated. Peer medians exclude Cesena. Core Peers are Forlì, Rimini, Ravenna, Imola, Carpi and Reggio nell'Emilia; the 13 cities add Faenza, Ferrara, Modena, Parma, Piacenza and Bologna.
- **Numbers.** Each quoted figure comes from `analysis/claims.py`, and `tests/studies/cesena/test_claims.py` fails if the text differs.

**In short.** Cesena has the strongest real income growth of the 13 cities and spends less than most of them, and most of its income lead predates 2019. Its headline job growth depends heavily on food manufacturing, temp agencies and cleaning, where the register shows breaks not yet understood. Its weak population growth, compared with the towns around it, comes mainly from more deaths than births. Whether residents move from Cesena to those towns because of housing costs remains an open question.

## Real economy

5. **Job growth above most peers, but sensitive to a few sectors** (`sensitive`, notebook 03; research track R1). Employment in active local units grew +14.78%<!-- c:emp_all_2024 --> in 2012–2024 (#4 of 13<!-- c:emp_all_2024_rank -->, +5,432<!-- c:net_jobs --> jobs). Temp agencies (ATECO 78, +1,704<!-- c:agency_jobs -->, 31%<!-- c:agency_jobs_share --> of the net gain) and food manufacturing (ATECO 10, +1,560<!-- c:food_jobs -->, 29%<!-- c:food_jobs_share -->) account for most of it. Food goes 3,402<!-- c:food_2021 -->, 1,490<!-- c:food_2022 -->, 4,938<!-- c:food_2023 -->, 4,252<!-- c:food_2024 --> in 2021–2024. The same kind of break appears elsewhere: the screen flags 83<!-- c:anomalies_n --> sector-years across the 13 cities.

   The food break was examined in research track R1. Its cause is not established, but the evidence points to a register effect, not a loss of jobs. What the data show: the province of Forlì-Cesena goes 7,310<!-- c:prov_food_2021 -->, 4,443<!-- c:prov_food_2022 -->, 7,977<!-- c:prov_food_2023 --> in 2021–2023, and it is the only province of Emilia-Romagna, 1 of 9<!-- c:prov_food_fall_n -->, whose food employment falls by more than a tenth in 2022 (the region moves -1.9%<!-- c:er_food_2022 -->). Cesena is 67%<!-- c:cesena_share_prov_drop --> of the province's drop. In Cesena the swing sits in units of 250 or more employees: their jobs go 2,576<!-- c:big_food_2021 -->, 560<!-- c:big_food_2022 -->, 4,057<!-- c:big_food_2023 --> in 2021–2023, with 2<!-- c:big_food_lu_2021 -->, 1<!-- c:big_food_lu_2022 -->, 2<!-- c:big_food_lu_2023 --> such units, while all other size classes together move +105<!-- c:rest_food_2022 --> in 2022. So it looks like two very large meat-processing units of one cooperative group, not the sector: one in Cesena and one in a neighbouring municipality of the province, which is missing from the register in 2022 and 2023 and back in 2024. FRAME-SBS industry jobs in Cesena dip in 2022 and recover in 2023, so a second ISTAT product built on the same register shows it too. Public documents (company filings as reported by registry aggregators, which were not checked against the filed accounts, and press releases) show no closure, sale or transfer of staff in those years, and a plant expansion with new hires announced in 2022. The evidence therefore points to a register classification or allocation effect for one cooperative, not to jobs lost. The mechanism is not established: the cooperative's activity code in the Chamber of Commerce registry has moved to the agricultural section at an unknown date, and agricultural enterprises are published outside this register; but a large food cooperative with the same kind of code appears to stay in the register, and the code is still agricultural in the years the units return. The historical Chamber of Commerce extract and the filed accounts would settle it; the public enterprise tables do not reach province level with ATECO 10. **No single series is preferred.** An imputed series would rest on a mechanism nobody has verified. The three variants in the table stay the way to read employment, and the headline rank of the full series should be read as one possible value, not the result, since the jobs are probably real but placed in the wrong years.
   
   Temp agencies and cleaning (ATECO 78, 81) show the same kind of move in 2024: together 2,748<!-- c:agcl_2023 --> and 2,855<!-- c:agcl_2024 --> jobs in 2023 and 2024, but the 250+ class gains +495<!-- c:agcl_big_2024 --> while the other classes lose -388<!-- c:agcl_rest_2024 -->. ## Limits across the study
- **One municipality, chosen in advance.** Rank proportions are not independent tests, and they were computed after Cesena's position was known.
- **Accounting break of 2016.** Levels, growth and rigidity are never compared across 2015–2016.
- **Cash is not accrual.** The cash series (SIOPE, 2017–2024) and the budget accruals (OpenBilanci and Finanza Locale, 2008–2021) are different measures: the cash rigidity is checked against the accrual ratio where both exist, never joined to it. No public per-municipality accrual source after 2021 was found (OpenBilanci stops at 2021; the OpenBDAP portal publishes only national and macro-area aggregates). For Cesena alone the Rendiconto files on the municipal transparency portal give the accrual ratio (committed personnel, interest and loan repayment over accrued current revenue): 2022 at 25.9%<!-- c:rigidity_accrual_2022 -->, 2023 at 27.9%<!-- c:rigidity_accrual_2023 --> and 2024 at 28.8%<!-- c:rigidity_accrual_2024 -->, within 1.9 points<!-- c:rigidity_accrual_cash_gap_max --> of the cash proxy in each year. The Comune's own Piano degli indicatori, whose rigidity indicator also counts IRAP and the pooled-fund adjustments and so differs in definition, prints 29.5%<!-- c:rigidity_official_ind_2023 --> for 2023 and 29.9%<!-- c:rigidity_official_ind_2024 --> for 2024. The 2022 figure is not comparable with the later two as published: until 2022 the Comune accrued the TARI waste levy (€15.27M<!-- c:tari_levy_2022 -->), and from 2023 the levy became a tariff billed by the operator, outside the budget. Without the levy in 2022 the ratio is 30.9%<!-- c:rigidity_accrual_ex_tari_2022 -->, and the change from 2022 to 2023 is -3.1 pp<!-- c:rigidity_accrual_ex_tari_change_22_23 --> instead of +2.0 pp<!-- c:rigidity_accrual_change_22_23 --> as published, within 1.1 points<!-- c:rigidity_accrual_ex_tari_cash_gap_2022 --> of the cash ex-TARI proxy for 2022. ## Income publication follow-up (November review)

The source-level follow-up distinguishes mean declared income from employment-and-assimilated income per recipient. The latter falls in real terms over the full window in Cesena while its recipient count grows; pension income per recipient rises. These are changing groups, not individual wage or pension trajectories. The primary study snapshot retains its original national HICP rate chain; direct annual index levels, including ECOICOP v2, give a lower positive mean-income change without changing the growth ranking. Component definitions are not reconciled to an exhaustive total, and local inflation has not been established.
