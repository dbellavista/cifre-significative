# Italy vs the EU27, 2005-2025: what the data show

_Living document: updated after each analysis round. See `docs/README.md` for the documentation map._

Sources: Eurostat, AMECO, IMF WEO, OECD, World Bank (see `docs/data_dictionary.md`). Charts are in `studies/italy_productivity/reports/figures/`, workings in `studies/italy_productivity/notebooks/`. All statements are descriptive associations on this panel; Most results run to 2024 or 2025 (stated in each entry); 2024-25 values can still be revised. Entries 1-58 are numbered; the "Statistical caution" block holds robustness checks and the notebooks (28-33, 35-36) whose results are orderings or nulls.

## Main differences
1. **Stagnation, not low income.** Real GDP per head is mid-table (11th-12th of 27, 12th in 2025) but grew +5.8 log points over 2005-2025 (+6.0%) vs +32.7 (+38.7%) for the median of the other members (to 2024: +5.2 vs +29.9; the median was +29.8 before Eurostat's release of 5 November 2026 revised Cyprus from +26.5 to +32.4: it is the midpoint of Cyprus and Czechia, +33.0, with Germany, +20.1, the next below); only Greece and Luxembourg (from a very high base) did worse. In purchasing-power terms Italy is 13th in 2025 (Cyprus moved ahead). The shortfall sits in the productivity/demography residual (+0.0 vs +22.7), not in hours or employment growth. 2025 is a first estimate. (02)
2. **Employment level.** 20-64 employment 67.6% vs 78.6% median in 2025 (2024: 67.1% vs 78.4%), female 58.0% vs 75.7%, both last in the EU27. (02, 06)
3. **Debt is an interest-and-growth problem, not a deficit one.** Primary balance averaged +0.1% of GDP (2005-24) vs -0.9% median, yet interest costs are 3.9% of GDP vs 1.3% median. Debt rose 29 pp in 2005-14 (interest +41 pp) and was flat in 2014-24 only because nominal growth offset +36 pp of interest. (03)
4. **Public investment** ran at 2.2-2.6% of GDP in 2012-19 (median 3.2-3.9%) and is 3.9% in 2025 (median 4.5%); total investment is now at the median (22.0% vs 21.9% in 2025). (03, 06)
5. **Oldest population, high pension cost.** Old-age dependency and median age are the highest in the EU27; old-age spending is 3 pp above what the other members' ageing relationship implies; 55-64 employment is 61.2% vs 69.5% in 2025 (2024: 59.0% vs 67.8%). Replacement rates are above the median (net 79% vs 72%) and the normal retirement age is set to reach 70. 65+ poverty is average. (04)
6. **Human capital lags, partly closing.** Tertiary attainment 25-34: 31.1% vs 44.5% in 2025 (26th; 2024: 31.6% vs 44.8%); NEET 13.3% vs 10.4% in 2025 (4th highest; 15.2% vs 9.9% and 2nd highest in 2024); early leavers nearly converged (8.2% vs 7.3% in 2025); PISA maths gap vs Germany shrank from 42 points (2006) to 4 (2022), mostly because German scores fell; Italy's maths score is 471 (2022), 19th of 26 EU members, while reading (482) is 8th. Real compensation per employee was 9.5% below 2005 in 2024 (vs +4-10% Germany/France/Spain). (05)
7. **A regional cluster.** South and Islands GDP per head is ~59% of the North's; unemployment 8-11% vs 3-4%; female employment 45% vs 67-70%. Dispersion is not unusual by EU standards because it is a broad split, not a capital-region outlier. (07)
8. **Productivity is a within-sector problem.** Output per hour in the nine NACE sections without real estate (public administration, education and health included) is 102 in 2025 (2005 = 100; 2025 is a first estimate) vs 128 for the median of the others; over 2005-2025 within-sector productivity added +2.5% vs +26.1% (26 members: Bulgaria has no 2025 value added yet; +21.9 on 25 members before Latvia's 2025 arrived on 29 October 2026, when the median was the midpoint of Cyprus, +17.6, and Hungary, +26.1), while reallocation of hours across the nine broad sectors explains little (+0.1 vs +3.5). Without public administration, education and health (output largely measured from inputs) the within-sector terms are +7.2 vs +29.4, Italy still third lowest of 26. Italy trails the median in all nine sectors (seven of nine over 2005-2019, with falls in construction and professional services; construction productivity rises after 2019, a period that contains the building-bonus years: a coincidence in time, not tested). (08)
9. **Governance and tax compliance.** Government effectiveness 71 in 2025 (median of others 76; 69 vs 75 in 2024), control of corruption 60 (median 64) and unchanged since 2005. The VAT compliance gap fell from 27.6% (2013) to 15.0% (2023) but is still ~2x the median (8.0%). Public procurement (Single Market Scoreboard, 2021-2024): single-bidder contracts 34% in 2024 against a 27% median (10th highest of 26), direct awards low (3% vs 5%), decisions slow (123 days vs 74, 4th slowest). A spending-waste measure is not in the data. (09)
10. **Spending is high in three places, not everywhere.** Total expenditure is 50.4% of GDP (2024) vs 46.6% median. Old-age pensions (+4.3 pp), debt interest (+2.6) and survivors' pensions (+1.5) account for the excess; education is 1.0 pp below, and the public-sector wage bill is 2.3 pp below the median. 2021-23 contain a large one-off jump in capital transfers (5-5.9% of GDP vs 1.0% in 2019), consistent with building tax-credit schemes. Revenue is also high (47.1% vs 43.2% in 2019), driven by direct taxes. In euro (Italy only, current prices) total spending went from EUR 705bn (2005) to 1,109bn (2024), +57% nominal; old-age pensions 175bn to 306bn; defence 28.3bn in 2024, 1.3% of GDP vs a 1.6% median (19th of 27, descriptive). (10)
11. **Gender and pay.** The employment gap between men and women is 19.1 pp (median 7.0), the largest in the EU27; 46.6% of part-time women want full-time work (median 18.0%); women are 29.0% of managers (median 37.3%). The unadjusted hourly pay gap (about 5%) looks small, but the overall earnings gap was 43.0% in 2018 (median 27.7%). Average-worker net pay in PPS was 114 vs 100 for the median of the other members but 72% of Germany's in 2023; Eurostat's average wage has a new basis from 2024 (break flag: 105 and 85% in 2025, a different measure, not a change). (11)

12. **Higher education.** Tertiary attainment of 25-34 year-olds was 31.6% in 2024 (median 44.8%; 26th of 27), women 38.5% and men 25.0% (2025: 31.1%, 37.8% and 24.7%, still 26th; the graduate statistics below are for 2024). STEM is 23.2% of graduates (median 24.4%) but ICT only 1.6% (median 5.7%, last); women are 39.5% of STEM graduates (4th highest). Bachelor graduates are older (27.7 vs 25.6; 24% are 30+), doctoral output is 1.6 per 1000 aged 25-34 (median 2.0), and 74.3% of recent graduates were employed in 2024 (median 86.2%; 75.8% vs 87.9% in 2025). Adult skills (PIAAC 2023): literacy 245 vs 254.5 median (16th of 19), 34.7% at level 1 or below (median 26.2%); down 5 points since 2012 like the median (-6); the shortfall is concentrated among graduates (271 vs 283.5) and the 25-34 cohort. (12)
13. **Productivity at 62-industry detail.** Within-industry productivity added 5.8% over 2005-2024 vs 18.2% (sum of medians; 2025 is not usable at this detail yet); reallocation across industries is not the story (1.0 vs 3.9). The 10 largest shortfalls explain 70% of the 12.4 pp (accommodation and food -1.5 pp, computers and electronics -1.2, agriculture -1.0). Construction was 2.5 pp of 11.6 in 2005-2019 (output per hour -1.4% a year) but is 0.8 pp of 12.4 with 2020-2024 included: a result of the period, not of the data revisions. (13)
14. **Ageing and debt arithmetic.** Gross public pension spending is projected to rise from 15.6% of GDP (2022) to 17.1% in 2040 before falling to 13.7% by 2070 (median of others 9.0% to 10.5%). Holding debt at 134% needs a primary surplus of about 1.3% of GDP if r-g is 1 pp; with the 2024 primary balance (+0.5%) and unoffset ageing costs debt reaches about 166% (2040) in an illustrative simulation, 139% with a 2% surplus. (14)
15. **Spending against outcomes.** Health outcomes are good for the spending: life expectancy 82.9 vs 80.4 predicted and treatable mortality 66.7 vs 97.5 predicted (2015-2019); education outcomes are close to what spending predicts; old-age poverty is slightly above what pension spending predicts. (15)
16. **Long history.** Real GDP per head grew 5.6% a year in the 1960s, 1.4% in the 1990s and about 0.1-0.2% in 2000-2019. A synthetic peer mix tracks Italy to 1994 and then pulls away by 8 log points (2005) and 19 (2024); Italy's divergence is the largest of 15 placebo units. (16)
17. **Pay and productivity.** Since 1995 output per hour is up 6.1% (Germany 33.3%) and real compensation per employee down 5.6% (Germany +14.8%); the decline in real pay is robust to the deflator (HICP vs GDP deflator, 2nd-3rd lowest in the EU27). (17)
18. **Childcare and taxes.** Formal childcare for under-3s (34.9%) is near the median and only weakly related to women's employment across countries; the tax system is lighter than peers on low single earners and heavier on high ones, and family benefits cut the net burden on households with children below the median. (18)
19. **2011 pension reform.** Relative to controls and placebo countries, employment of 55-64 year-olds rose 5.8 pp (placebo median +0.2) and of 60-64 year-olds 8.2 pp; youth outcomes cannot be attributed because of the simultaneous recession. (19)
20. **Regions and robustness.** In 2019 the South's GDP per head was 56% of the North's: 42% of the log gap is lower output per hour, 51% fewer hours per inhabitant. The growth regressions are not robust: the earlier significant investment coefficient depended on including Ireland. (20)

21. **Crime and migration.** All figures are for 2024. Homicide victims fell to 0.57 per 100,000 (median 0.89; among the lowest), robbery and theft are about twice the median, prisons are at 122% of capacity; registered immigration is 7.7 per 1000 (median 13.8) but first-time asylum applicants are 256 per 100,000 (median 87; 218 vs 70 in 2025), and net migration of citizens aged 25-34 is -6.9 per 1000, the lowest of the 15 countries that report it. (21)
22. **Governments and policies.** Italy grew more slowly than the median of the other members under 21 of 22 governments since 1989 (D'Alema II is counted with D'Alema I; the exception being Draghi's post-pandemic rebound): the shortfall spans coalitions. Timing evidence for pensions, the 2012 ECB announcements, tax compliance, immigration laws and the Superbonus is shown against 46 dated policies (all confirmed against official or legal sources); no causal attribution. (22)

23. **Growth accounting.** Italy's labour productivity growth gap is a TFP gap: the TFP contribution is -0.36 pp a year in 1996-2019 (Germany +0.85, median of the 15 other members with EU KLEMS TFP data +0.79; six of them cover only part of the years) while labour composition and capital contributions are near the median; TFP in the market economy is 8% below its 1995 level. By industry the largest gaps are in manufacturing (-0.43 vs +1.55), utilities, construction and professional services. Italy's tax gap (MEF): EUR 95.6bn (2018) to 90.9bn (2022), 19.7% to 17.0% of tax due, with self-employed income tax at about 60% propensity. (23, 09)

24. **Correlation discovery** (notebook 24, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). About 1,000 indicator pairs pass false-discovery control in the raw 2015-19 cross-section, about 260 once log GDP per head is removed (206 before the GDP revisions of 5 November 2026: the count is sensitive to the income control), and 0 survive a shuffled-country null; the top pairs are mostly definitional, so the list needs curation. Of 13 literature hypotheses tested with country and year fixed effects, 9 are significant and 7 go the expected way; public investment vs budget balance and pension vs education spending go the other way. An exploratory check (2026-11-11, outside the pre-specified design): all nine also hold in first differences, but with log real GDP per head as a control three of the seven lose significance (growth-debt p 0.20, pensions-NEET p 0.12, pensions-55-64 employment p 0.15) and pensions-education is borderline (p 0.052): these are ratios to GDP, so part of the association is the shared denominator and the cycle, which year effects remove only when it is common to all countries. Female employment predicts GDP per head (not the reverse) at 1-3 years; for childcare vs female employment the reverse direction dominates. Italy's NEET rate is 2.6 residual SDs above the line implied by pension spending and by early school leaving. Associations and predictive order only.

25. **Regional correlation discovery** (notebook 25, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). With about 230 NUTS2 regions and 26 indicators, 165 within-country pairs survive false-discovery control in 2015-19 (0 discoveries after shuffling regions within countries), mostly labour-market pairs; cross-theme survivors include AROPE-unemployment (rho 0.73) and early leaving-NEET (0.71). Retested with the within-country permutation p, 164 of the 165 levels survivors and 53 of the 59 changes survivors remain (the dropped pairs are weak, |rho| 0.14-0.21). Of 10 pre-specified regional hypotheses with region and year effects, 5 are significant at q < 0.05, 4 in the expected direction (early leaving-NEET, income-net migration, output-pay per hour, unemployment-investment); broadband-output goes the other way. Cross-sectional AROPE links vanish once region effects are added. Italy has the steepest slope of 14-18 countries for graduate share vs output per hour and vs female employment, and for early leaving vs NEET, concentrated in the South and Islands (placebo p of 0.06-0.07, the smallest the ranking allows). Regional income does not converge in Italy (beta +0.47, p = 0.21) while Germany does (-1.13, p < 0.001). Associations only.

26. **High-frequency dynamics** (notebook 26, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). With 14 monthly and 4 quarterly series (2005-2026) all tests run on stationary transformations. The Italy-Germany spread peaked at 5.19 pp in November 2011 and is 0.89 pp in September 2026; its monthly changes are hardly explained by the previous month's differentials in sentiment, unemployment, core inflation or industrial growth (R2 1-16%, nothing survives false-discovery control, same for Spain, France, Portugal, Greece). In panel Granger tests sentiment and industry confidence help predict industrial production one month ahead (and the reverse), industrial production leads the change in unemployment only at 6 months, GDP growth leads employment and hours by 1-2 quarters. Italy's positive production-unemployment correlation (r = 0.34) is a 2020 artefact; without March 2020 to June 2021 production leads unemployment by 6 months (r = -0.16). Wage pass-through of inflation is +0.44 pp per point in 2005-2021 (Italy 0.31, 19th of 27) and undetectable in the 2021-23 energy shock. None of 218 event-window tests (Italian governments, policies, ECB decisions) survives false-discovery control (16 at p < 0.05). Predictive order and association only.

27. **Daily spread and rating changes** (notebook 27, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). Around 93 dated events (Italian governments, policies, ECB decisions and 40 rating or outlook changes by S&P, Moody's, Fitch and DBRS) the daily Italy-Germany spread shows no move that survives false-discovery control (279 tests, 17 with p < 0.05 against about 14 expected, smallest q 0.163). The largest is the 9 March 2020 lockdown (+0.83 pp over five trading days); four of 15 ECB-related tests narrow the spread (PEPP -0.81 pp at one day, "whatever it takes" -0.65, OMT -0.57) with smallest q 0.430. Rating changes show no consistent move: pooled with the expected sign, the one-day mean signed z is 0.09 (p 0.29, 25 of 40 in the expected direction; q 0.63 over the 6 pooled tests); an earlier one-day hint (p 0.021 with 14 partly verified dates) disappeared when verified dates were added and has not returned. All 40 dates are now checked against the agencies' own records (Moody's, Fitch, DBRS and S&P pages or documents; one date corrected: Moody's outlook change 2022-08-05, not 08-08; twelve rows added), and the earlier tilt among aggregator-only S&P dates (z 1.32, p 0.015, three events) cannot be tested any more. A shuffled-date null gives 0.02 discoveries per repetition. Holders of the debt (D4, descriptive): since 2021-Q1 financial corporations' holdings of Italian government debt fell from EUR 1,535bn to 1,242bn and the central bank's from 671bn to 512bn (ECB SHSS); banks hold at most EUR 424bn (government plus non-financial corporate debt, 2026-Q2), and EBA exposures of 9-12 large banks (EUR 243-291bn, 2021-06 to 2025-06) are 0.66-0.70 of that on all 9 common dates. Eurosystem purchases (descriptive): the PSPP plus PEPP stock of Italian debt (cumulative net purchases, amortised cost, the Treasury's basis: PSPP 312.2bn and PEPP 246.8bn at end-2025) peaked at EUR 739.3bn in July 2022 and was 497.7bn in September 2026; average maturity of the holdings 7.4 years. Peer spreads (Italy minus Spain, France, Portugal; same 93 dates): 16, 16 and 18 of 279 tests with p < 0.05, smallest q 0.22-0.63 within a peer and 0.65-0.70 over all 837; pooled rating changes give a one-day mean signed z of 0.33 (Spain, q 0.085 within Spain, 0.254 over the 18 peer rows), 0.09 (France), -0.01 (Portugal), against 0.09 for Germany; shuffled-date nulls 0.01-0.025 discoveries per repetition. Greece (descriptive): no consistent widening, the pooled sign is the opposite (-0.26 at one day over 40 changes). Limits: the peer families share Italy's yield and events with the primary one, yields from a commercial aggregator, rating dates checked against the agencies' own records (Scope not covered), anticipation of scheduled reviews. (27)

28. **Why the South** (notebook 34, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). Within countries, regions with better perceived governance (EQI 2017) have higher employment (rho 0.36) and lower unemployment (-0.41) in all four waves, and this holds beyond income (0.45 and -0.475) but not for output per hour (-0.07); Italy's EQI slope is like the other countries'. Local units per 1,000 inhabitants, the manufacturing share and pay per person employed are steeper against employment in Italy than in any of 14-17 other countries. Across Italy's 21 regions, net migration and central-government spending follow the North-South split (rho with employment 0.90 and 0.67, falling to 0.41 and 0.01 within zones), while capital and local-government spending keep their association (0.49 and 0.62). Associations only: perceptions follow prosperity as well as precede it, migration follows jobs, and the Italy-only part has 21 regions in one cluster. (34)

29. **NUTS 2021 geography and spatial dependence** (notebook 37, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). Translating region codes to NUTS 2021 (303 codes to 242 regions; 56 renamed, 7 splits and 7 post-2021 codes left out) leaves the notebook 34 EU-wide result unchanged (same survivors in every wave except one in 2010; no flips in the primary wave). The regional columns are spatially autocorrelated (Moran's I 0.52-0.65 for unemployment, employment, EQI and AROPE within countries), and the within-country permutation test over-rejects when x is spatially smooth (p < 0.05 in 10.7% of tests at Moran's I 0.16 and 25.0% at 0.57, with 0.43 and 4.08 false discoveries per family; the spatially filtered pipeline gives 6.5-6.6%). After eigenvector filtering the 2017 family without the income control shrinks from 37 to 15 survivors (no EQI pair among them except impartiality against unemployment and hours), the beyond-income family goes from 18 to 23, and Italy's steep slopes of notebook 34 (6 of 40 pairs at the smallest possible rank) fall to 1 pair at p_rank <= 0.10 (a different one): the plain EQI-employment associations and Italy's steepness are partly shared North-South patterns. Filtering also removes the North-South gradient that "why the South" is about, so both versions are reported. The notebook 25 pair screens, rerun on the NUTS 2021 geography and with spatial filtering (`experiments.run_spatial_screens`), hold up: 164 and 53 BH survivors on the original codes (levels, changes), 169 and 57 on NUTS 2021, 159 and 46 after filtering with 152 and 44 in common; the pairs lost are weak (median |rho| 0.18 and 0.25) and no kept pair changes sign. The plain whole-screen null makes 17 (levels) and 13 (changes) false discoveries per simulation when independent noise has Moran's I of about 0.57 and none at 0.16, the filtered screen none in either case. Descriptive only.

30. **How the budget laws were financed** (notebook 38, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). From the resources-and-uses tables of the Camera/Senate budget-service dossiers (2016-2026; 2022 gross split rebuilt from the measure lines of the Senate budget service's note, coverage 0.37; the 2020-21 pandemic decrees apart): between 2016 and 2024 the bills were covered by 49-65% inside the law except in 2021 (0.20) and 2022 (0.37), so in the other years 35-51% was financed by deficit; 2025 (0.74) and 2026 (0.96) are the most covered, but their coverage is concentrated in the first year (three-year ratios 0.61 and 0.79). The planned deficit effect is 0.39-1.34% of GDP; lower spending is the larger source of coverage in 2016, 2017 and 2025, higher revenue in 2018 and 2026. The 2020 pandemic decrees covered 0.09 (deficit effect 6.3% of GDP). No link with the realised deficit or debt can be shown: 0 of 6 pre-specified tests survive (smallest q 0.186; 0.365 before the 2022 gross split was rebuilt), all six rho negative (-0.14 to -0.64, the opposite of the expected sign, driven by the timing of the nominal-growth shock), the year-shuffle null gives a discovery in 6% of shuffles, the lead placebo finds nothing. About ten years: only |rho| of 0.65 or more can be told from zero. Against the deficit target of each draft budgetary plan (outturn minus target: +0.2, +0.2, +0.6 in 2016-18, -0.9 in 2019, +7.2 in 2020, +1.9, +2.5, +2.6 in 2021-23, -0.9 and -0.2 in 2024-25) the manovra's planned effect and deficit-financed share are positively but not significantly associated (rho 0.29 and 0.49, q 0.41 and 0.32; 0.07 and 0.35 without 2020-21). The misses, split into euro-level misses of revenue, primary expenditure and interest plus a GDP-denominator term: 2020 (+7.2) is 3.3 points of revenue shortfall and 3.9 of primary-expenditure overrun; in 2021-2023 (+1.9, +2.5, +2.6) primary expenditure ran 3.5-3.8 points above plan every year, partly offset by revenue above plan; nominal growth beat the forecast in those years and, outside 2020-21, the miss was larger when it did (rho 0.74, p 0.043, n 8; exploratory, outside the pre-specified family). By economic category the 2021-2023 overruns are almost entirely capital transfers (ESA D.9: +3.4, +3.1, +4.2 points of GDP, 89%, 61% and 118% of the total expenditure miss, interest included: in 2022 interest ran 1.35 points above plan), consistent with the Eurostat/Istat treatment of the Superbonus and bonus facciate credits as payable tax credits recorded as capital expenditure (AGI, 1 March 2023; 2021 deficit revised from 7.2% to 9.0%), though D.9 also holds other transfers; the 2020 miss splits between capital transfers (+1.5), social payments (+1.3) and subsidies (+0.6). Figures are the bills as first presented (for 2019 the approved text has about half the initial deficit effect; the 2023 bill is attributed to the Meloni government). Descriptive only.

31. **Contract timing and the real wage loss of the 2021-23 shock** (notebook 39, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). Istat's contractual hourly wages for 113 agreement groups (80 leaf sectors, 78 with data): 69 of 78 had a lower real contractual wage in December 2024 than in December 2021 (67 in 2025; cumulative inflation 14.3% and 15.7%), median real change -6.0% (private) and -6.2% (public). Across the 30 parent groups, those whose last increment was oldest at December 2021 lost more (rho -0.58 for the months since the last step, -0.52 for the months without a step in the previous 24; p about 0.001, q 0.001; private groups only -0.60 and -0.58); the calm-period placebo (2016-19) is flat (-0.06, +0.06) and the shuffled null gives a discovery in 4.5% of shuffles. The association is between parent groups (sub-sectors share the agreements: within parents p 0.5-0.83, and the unit was changed to the parent groups after the leaf-level results were seen). Private-sector waiting time for renewals (all employees) went from 11.1 months (December 2021) to 16.6 (2023) then 4.1 (2024). 30 clusters, no employee weights, Ateco sectors rather than agreements; associations only. **Qualified by entry 33** (renewal dates and sector deflator): much of this association is a difference between sectors.

33. **Renewal dates from CNEL's archive and a sector deflator** (notebook 39, section E, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). The CNEL open-data archive gives the signing date and expiry of every renewal; 74 of the 78 leaf groups were mapped by hand to one contract (53 `verified`, 21 `partly verified`; four groups have no collective contract). On the 24 parent groups of the verified leaves, the months the contract had been expired at December 2021 go with a larger real loss to 2024 (rho -0.54, q 0.022, one of four tests), the months since the last renewal less (-0.36, q 0.17), nothing survives against 2025; the staircase proxy on the same groups gives -0.36 (q 0.18), so the -0.58 of the 30 parents does not carry over, and the proxy agrees only moderately with the dates (rho 0.44 and 0.64). The 2016-19 placebo is not flat for the expiry (-0.39, p 0.084, q 0.17). **The stale agreements are the service and public ones**: mean staleness 9.9 months for the 15 goods parents, 34.1 for the 11 services and 28.2 for the 4 public ones; real change -3.9%, -7.5%, -6.6%. With a sector deflator (goods 16.0%, services 11.3%, all-items 14.3%) the proxy against the 2024 outcome falls to -0.14 (q 0.45) and the renewal dates to about zero; with the national deflator and x shuffled within sector the rho are unchanged but no test survives (q 0.063-0.075; expiry q 0.34). The age of an agreement cannot be separated from its sector in these data. Limits: my mapping labels are not an independent second source, the sector deflator uses consumer prices, 24-30 groups, no employee weights (CNEL's `dipendenti` field is empty; a CNEL-INPS workers file is a lead).

34. **Italy's deficit surprise ranked among the EU27** (notebook 41, `docs/analysis_framework.md`). Reference: the European Commission's autumn forecast of the year before (statistical annexes of autumn 2016-2025, all members, `sources/ec_forecasts.py`; budget years 2017-2025). Surprise = outturn minus forecast, in % of GDP, for net borrowing and its parts. In 2021-23 Italy's deficit was above the forecast by 2.30 points on average (median of the other 26: -1.25); after removing the other members' yearly median it is 3.58, the largest of the 27 (next: Hungary 2.88, Latvia 2.28; one-sided placebo p = 1/27 = 0.037, also first among the 20 euro-area members). It does not survive false-discovery control over the 16 pre-specified tests (smallest q 0.254). The rank holds in 2022 and 2023 singly (first), when one year of the window is left out (rank 1 or 2) and against the forecast of the budget year itself (first) or of two years before (second); outside the window Italy is unremarkable (2017-19 rank 10, 2024-25 rank 15). The overrun is spread over revenue shortfall (rank 5), primary expenditure (rank 3) and interest (rank 3), none of them first: the sum is what is unusual. The Commission's forecast and the government's own target differ by 0.13 points on average, so the miss is similar against either. With the Commission's own estimate of the year just ended (autumn forecast of B+1) as the outturn instead of the latest vintage, 2021-23 is still first (raw +1.73, idiosyncratic +3.02) and the whole period second; Italy's 2021 deficit was revised up by 1.7 points (the others' median -0.1, largest +0.2), the building-credit treatment, and no other year moves by more than 0.2.
